# Blue Ocean Strategy

## The idea
Kim and Mauborgne use two images. A red ocean is the market as it exists today: boundaries are set and firms fight over the same buyers. A blue ocean is demand no one is serving yet, a space with little or no competition. The authors argue that instead of beating rivals inside the existing market, a business can make rivals beside the point by giving buyers a jump in value while keeping costs low. They call this value innovation, and present it as a way out of the usual choice between being different and being cheap.

Two tools make it concrete. The strategy canvas is a one-page chart. Along the bottom you list the factors the industry competes on (price, speed, range, ambiance, whatever applies). Up the side you score how much of each factor buyers receive. Plotting yourself and your competitors gives each a value curve, and the usual finding is that the curves look alike. The four actions framework then asks four questions to redraw your curve: which factors the industry takes for granted should be eliminated, which should be reduced well below the standard, which should be raised well above it, and which new factors should be created that the industry has never offered. The answers go into a grid called ERRC.

The authors say all four questions matter: eliminating and reducing cut cost, raising and creating lift buyer value.

## When to use it
- When the market is crowded and every competitor offers the same bundle.
- When the founder cannot explain why a customer would choose them over the obvious alternatives.
- When deciding which features, services or hours to drop to fund the one thing customers truly value.

## Questions to put to the founder
1. List the five things every competitor in your category offers. Which of them do customers actually value?
2. Which of those could you drop without most customers noticing?
3. Which one factor will you deliver far better than the standard?
4. What have you seen customers ask for that nobody in the category offers?
5. Who is in your area and could use this product but does not, and what stops them?
6. If you drew your offer and your nearest rival's on the same chart, would the lines look different?

## Red flags
- Every factor on the founder's list is raised, with nothing eliminated or reduced.
- The new offer is a long list of extras with no focus.
- The founder calls the market blue but has no evidence that any buyers exist for it.
- The cost of the new factors is higher than buyers will pay, and nothing offsets it.
- The idea is new only in the founder's description; the value curve matches a rival's.

## How the pack uses it
founder-competitors draws a simple strategy canvas of the founder and three to five rivals and stores the factor scores in founder/competitors.md. founder-offer runs the four questions and writes the ERRC grid into founder/offer.md. founder-board uses the canvas as evidence for the differentiation lens, and founder-brand picks the one raised factor as the promise.

Example: a founder opens a small gym. Competitors all compete on equipment range, class variety and low monthly price. The ERRC grid: eliminate towel service; reduce equipment range to what a 30-minute workout needs; raise coaching attention per member; create a booking app that fits shift workers' hours. The founder then tests whether shift workers will pay for it, using founder-interview.

## Limits
- The book's case studies were selected after the strategies had worked, so it cannot say how often similar moves fail.
- Creating new demand is costly and uncertain; most small businesses will gain more from a sharper position within an existing market.
- The claim that industry structure can be changed by choice sits against Porter's view that structure limits profit; an independent study comparing the two approaches was published in Harvard Business Review in 2010.
- It supplies a method for exploring options, not a way to prove demand exists.
