# Anchoring and Loss Aversion

## The idea
Two findings from the research of Kahneman and Tversky, popularised in Thinking, Fast and Slow.

Anchoring: when people estimate a number, they are pulled toward any number they have just seen, even an irrelevant one. In the book's best-known experiment, a wheel of fortune rigged to stop at 10 or 65 was spun before people estimated the share of African nations in the United Nations. The average answers were 25 percent after 10 and 45 percent after 65. The same pull shows up in price judgements, including by professionals, in a real estate study the book cites (Northcraft and Neale, 1987). For a seller: the first price a buyer sees becomes a reference point for judging every price after it.

Loss aversion, part of prospect theory (1979): a loss weighs more than a gain of the same size. The book says that in experiments the ratio is usually in the range of 1.5 to 2.5, averaged over people; some are more loss averse than others. A buyer also compares to a reference point, so what counts as a gain or a loss depends on what they expected to pay or keep.

Used honestly, this means: show the true standard price first, where it is relevant; frame real savings as real savings; and make the cost of doing nothing (the true cost, not an invented one) visible.

## When to use it
- Setting a price list with a basic, standard and premium option.
- Writing an offer that includes a free trial, a guarantee or a refund.
- Deciding whether to show a "was" price when a sale ends.
- Explaining the price of a service to a hesitant buyer.
- Thinking about why customers resist a price rise.

## Questions to put to the founder
- What price will a customer see first, on the page or in the conversation?
- Is that price one you have actually charged to real customers?
- What do customers pay now for the alternative?
- Which option do you want most customers to choose, and why?
- What does the customer lose by not buying, in money or time you can show?
- Is there a guarantee that removes the fear of losing their money?
- When did a customer last say "too expensive", and compared with what?

## Red flags
- A crossed-out "was" price the shop never charged.
- A premium tier added only to make the standard one look cheap, with no buyers.
- Countdown timers or "only two left" that are untrue.
- A price that looks reasonable only against an unrelated, made-up reference.
- Claims of loss ("you will lose out") that are exaggerated.

## How the pack uses it
founder-pricing uses the order of options and the reference price when presenting the range from the buyer panel and records the chosen anchor and its evidence in `founder/pricing.md`. founder-offer frames guarantees and risk reversal as removing a feared loss, in `founder/offer.md`. founder-marketing checks that every comparison and every sale price is real. The pack forbids fake anchors: any reference price must be one actually charged or a true market price the founder can show.

Example: a tutor offers three monthly packages. The premium package is a real service, with real hours, priced fairly. The mid package is the one most parents need. Each is described with what the parent gets, and the sheet says honestly what a single hour costs. If the premium never sells, that is fine, but it must exist.

## Limits
- Anchoring is well replicated, but the exact size varies; do not expect a fixed effect from any price.
- Loss aversion has been challenged: Gal and Rucker (2018) argue its evidence is weaker and more context dependent than often claimed.
- The book's wider claims about the mind come from lab tasks, not shops; test in real sales.
- Anchors do not rescue a bad offer, and manipulative use damages trust and may break consumer protection rules; founder-compliance should check price claims.
