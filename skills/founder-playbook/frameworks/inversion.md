# Inversion and the Pre-Mortem

## The idea
Two related habits, one from an investor and one from a research psychologist.

Inversion: instead of asking how to succeed, ask what would make this fail, and then avoid those things. Charlie Munger used this habit often and credited the mathematician Carl Jacobi with the instruction to invert. It is recorded in Poor Charlie's Almanack (2005).

The pre-mortem: Gary Klein described it in Harvard Business Review in September 2007. The team imagines the project has already failed, then each person writes down the reasons it failed. The article argues that people are often reluctant to voice doubts during planning, and that imagining failure as a fact makes it safe, even expected, to speak up. Because the failure is imagined as already happened, the reasons come out more concretely.

A simple procedure, based on how the technique is described in later research (Veinott and colleagues, 2010): get familiar with the plan; imagine it has completely failed; each person writes the reasons on their own; combine the lists; then go back to the plan and decide what to change or watch for.

## When to use it
- A plan is nearly final and the founder is feeling confident.
- Before signing a lease, taking a loan or hiring the first employee.
- After a business plan is compiled, as a last check.
- When a partner or family member has doubts and has not said them.
- When a founder has trouble naming risks beyond "no customers".

## Questions to put to the founder
- It is twelve months from now and the business has closed. What happened?
- Which three causes of that failure would be your fault?
- Which causes would be outside your control?
- What was the first warning sign you ignored?
- What would you need to see in month three to know it is going wrong?
- Which cause can you remove this week?
- What is your plan if the top cause happens?

## Red flags
- The founder cannot list more than one failure cause.
- Every cause is external (the economy, competitors) and none is the founder's own.
- Failure causes are vague: "marketing didn't work".
- The founder gets defensive or treats doubts as disloyal.
- The same risk appears in the pre-mortem as in the plan but has no mitigation.

## How the pack uses it
founder-board runs a pre-mortem lens after the other lenses and writes a ranked failure list into `founder/board.md`. founder-plan puts the top causes into the risk section and adds mitigations. founder-launch turns the early-warning signs into things to check in the first 30 days. founder-ops turns operational causes into routines.

Example: a founder plans an online shop for handmade candles. Pre-mortem output: suppliers late in the holiday season; shipping costs eat the margin; ads cost more than the first order is worth; the founder burns out packing orders. Mitigations: a second wax supplier, a free-shipping threshold set from real costs, a cap on ad spend until repeat orders appear, a packing partner once orders pass a weekly level the founder sets.

## Limits
- It finds risks you can imagine, not the unforeseen ones.
- It runs best with a group; a solo founder must write alone and then ask someone else to add causes.
- Klein's article is a practitioner essay, not a controlled study of outcomes.
- Inversion alone gives only a list of things to avoid, not a business; combine it with a positive plan.
- Too much of it can freeze a founder; limit the session to a short, fixed time.
