# The Seven Principles of Influence

## The idea
Cialdini's book asks why people say yes to a request, and answers with a small set of recurring rules of human behaviour. The original edition and the revised edition carry six principles. The New and Expanded edition (2021) adds a seventh, unity, according to the author's own site. If you cite the count, name the edition.

The seven are: reciprocity, scarcity, authority, commitment and consistency, liking, social proof and unity. In short:

- Reciprocity: people feel obliged to return what someone has given them.
- Scarcity: things that are rare or running out seem more valuable.
- Authority: people defer to those they take to be knowledgeable.
- Commitment and consistency: people want to act in line with what they have said and done before.
- Liking: people agree more readily with those they like.
- Social proof: people decide what is right by seeing what others do.
- Unity: shared identity, "one of us", which is different from simple likeness.

Cialdini also writes about spotting these tactics when used on you. That defensive reading is the one that keeps the framework honest: a principle becomes manipulation when what it signals is false.

## When to use it
- Writing an offer and deciding what proof to show.
- Preparing a first outreach message to a stranger.
- Designing a free sample or a trial.
- Checking an ad for claims that are really tactics.
- Building a referral or loyalty habit.

## Questions to put to the founder
- What do you give a prospect for free before asking for money, and does it cost you something real?
- Is any limit on stock, seats or dates a real one?
- Which credentials or results can the customer verify?
- What small first step could a customer take that makes a larger purchase natural?
- Which real customers will let you quote them by name?
- What do your best customers have in common with each other and with you?
- How many people have bought, and can you show that number honestly?

## Red flags
- Fake scarcity: invented countdowns or "only three left" with unlimited stock.
- Made-up or unattributed testimonials, or purchased reviews.
- Credentials or press that do not exist.
- A free gift whose real purpose is to make refusal feel rude.
- A small commitment obtained by trickery (a pre-ticked box, a trial that renews without notice).
- Unity claimed with a group the founder does not belong to.

## How the pack uses it
founder-offer maps each element of the offer stack to the principle it relies on and asks for the real evidence, saved in `founder/offer.md`. founder-marketing uses the principles for the 30-day campaign and checks each claim in `founder/marketing.md`. founder-sales uses reciprocity and social proof in outreach. founder-brand uses liking and unity for voice.

Example: a local physiotherapist. Reciprocity: a free ten-minute posture check. Authority: the registration and years of practice, stated plainly. Social proof: three named, consenting clients and the real number treated. Scarcity: only real appointment slots, shown as they are. Commitment: a short first session priced low, then a plan. Every item is true and checkable.

## Limits
- The research base is mostly experiments and the author's observation of sales trainers; effect sizes in a shop are unknown and need testing.
- Some psychology findings from this era have not replicated well, so treat each as a hypothesis.
- Principles are shortcuts for hurried decisions; for big purchases, buyers use more evidence.
- Do not use any principle to push a customer into a purchase they do not need. Consumer-protection law applies to fake scarcity and fake reviews, so involve founder-compliance.
