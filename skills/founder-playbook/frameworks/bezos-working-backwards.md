# Working Backwards

## The idea
Working Backwards is a way of vetting a new product or service that Amazon used from the mid 2000s. Instead of starting from what the company can already make and then looking for buyers, you start from the buyer's experience and work back to what has to be built. The authors, two former Amazon executives, describe it in their book and on their companion site.

The main tool is a short document called the PR/FAQ: a press release plus a list of frequently asked questions, written before anything is built. The release is written as if the product has launched. It has a title, a one-line subtitle naming the specific customer and the benefit, a summary with a launch date, a paragraph on the customer's problem, a paragraph on the solution, a note on how it differs from what the customer uses today, a quote from the company, a quote from a customer, and how to get started. The authors say a product for everyone is a warning sign.

The FAQ has two parts. The external part answers what a customer would ask (price, how it works, support, returns). The internal part answers the hard questions behind the scenes: unit economics, upfront investment, dependencies, legal issues, what must be true for success, and the top reasons it might fail. The framework treats the document as a way to find truth, not to sell the idea, and says a first draft should take hours, not weeks.

## When to use it
- You have an idea and want to test whether you can describe it in a way a stranger would want.
- You keep adding features or services and cannot say who the offer is for.
- Before spending on a logo, a shop fit-out or stock.
- When two or three ideas compete and you need a cheap way to rank them.

## Questions to put to the founder
- Who exactly is the customer, in one sentence a stranger could picture?
- What problem do they have today, described in their words, not yours?
- What do they use now to solve it, and what does that cost them?
- In what way is yours better, cheaper or faster than that?
- What is the price, and how does a customer get started?
- What is the hardest thing you must solve before you can deliver this?
- What are the top three reasons this might not work?

## Red flags
- The customer is described as everyone, or as a long list of types.
- The press release lists features but no problem.
- The only difference from competitors is the founder's passion or "quality".
- The founder cannot say what customers do today instead.
- The FAQ avoids price, cost or risk.

## How the pack uses it
founder-launch asks the founder for a one-page PR/FAQ before choosing the cheap real test, and saves it in `founder/launch.md`. founder-offer reuses the problem and solution paragraphs as the base of the offer stack in `founder/offer.md`. founder-brand checks that the promise matches the headline.

Example: a solo bookkeeper plans a monthly service for small cafes. One page: headline naming the service, subtitle "for cafe owners with under ten staff who lose Sunday nights to receipts", a problem paragraph about late tax filings, a solution paragraph, a line on what cafes use today (spreadsheets, an occasional accountant), one owner quote, a price and a sign-up step. FAQ: what happens if receipts are late, what it costs the founder in time per client, and why a cafe would leave its current accountant. If the founder cannot answer the last one, the idea goes back for rework before any marketing spend.

## Limits
- Built for a large company deciding among many product bets; the multi-meeting review is more than a small business needs. Keep the writing, drop the process.
- The release is a hypothesis. It does not replace talking to real customers, and a persuasive document can hide a weak idea.
- Written by insiders who describe their own company's success, so the book carries survivorship bias.
