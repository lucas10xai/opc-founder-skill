# Default Alive or Default Dead, and Ramen Profitable

## The idea
Two essays give a pair of survival tests. The first asks: if expenses stay flat and revenue keeps growing at the pace of recent months, does the business reach profit before the cash runs out? If yes, it is default alive. If no, it is default dead, and the first conversation must be about changing the trajectory. The essay says many founders do not know which they are, and that asking too early is much safer than too late.

It names a dangerous combination, the fatal pinch: default dead, slow growth, and too little time to fix it. It warns against hoping investors will rescue the company. Even with strong growth, fundraising is only a first plan, so write down a second plan and the date to switch. It calls hiring too fast the biggest killer of startups that raise money, since a moderately appealing product does not grow faster because more people are hired.

The second essay defines ramen profitable: the business earns just enough to pay the founders' living costs. The point is time, not success. You no longer need to raise money to survive, you negotiate from strength, and you avoid fundraising distraction. Its example is about 3,000 dollars of revenue a month, and it warns that this can slide into consulting while claiming to be a product company.

Our working test is arithmetic, not a formula from the essay: list monthly costs including your own living costs, take current monthly revenue and recent monthly growth, project month by month until revenue meets costs, and add up the shortfall. If it is below the cash on hand, the business is default alive.

## When to use it
- A business has run a few months and the founder cannot say how long the money lasts.
- The founder plans to hire, rent premises or buy stock before turning a profit.
- The plan counts on a loan, investor or grant to cover a gap.
- Someone is deciding when to leave a job to run the business.

## Questions to put to the founder
1. How much cash do you have today?
2. What are your total monthly costs, including your own living costs?
3. What was monthly revenue for each of the last three to six months?
4. At that growth rate, in which month does revenue reach costs?
5. Is the total shortfall until then less than your cash?
6. If you could raise or borrow nothing, what would you do, and by what date?
7. What monthly revenue covers your own living costs?

## Red flags
- The founder cannot state cash, costs and recent revenue from memory.
- The survival plan is "raise or borrow more".
- Hiring or fit-out is planned while growth is slow.
- Revenue is flat while costs rise.
- No written plan B or switch date.

## How the pack uses it
founder-cfo takes cash, costs and recent revenue, projects month by month at the observed growth and at half that growth, and writes the verdict and break-even month into founder/cfo.md. founder-plan carries the default alive or dead verdict into its headline. founder-metrics repeats the check with real numbers after launch.

Example: a small print-on-demand shop has 12,000 in cash, 4,000 monthly costs, 1,500 revenue this month and 20 percent monthly growth. Revenue passes costs in month 7 with a total shortfall near 9,100, so it is default alive. At 10 percent growth the shortfall passes 12,000 around month 6, so it is default dead. The finding is how fragile the first answer is.

## Limits
- Written for venture-funded startups with fast growth. A shop or cafe with steady, seasonal revenue does not grow exponentially, so extrapolating recent months can mislead.
- The projection assumes flat costs, which rarely holds as a business grows.
- Ramen profitable is a milestone, not a destination, as the essay itself says.
- The advice comes from one investor's view of startups he funded.
