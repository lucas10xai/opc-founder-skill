# Hedgehog Concept and Flywheel

## The idea
Collins borrows an old contrast, popularised by Isaiah Berlin, between a fox that knows many tricks and a hedgehog that knows one big thing. His claim is that the companies he studied that did best were run by people who found one clear idea and stuck to it. He calls this the Hedgehog Concept and places it where three circles overlap: what you care about most deeply, what you could be better at than anyone else, and what powers your economics (he calls this the economic or resource engine). The key point is that it is an understanding of what you can actually be best at, and equally of what you cannot, not a goal or a plan to be the best.

The flywheel is the second idea. Collins pictures a heavy wheel pushed in one consistent direction: early turns are slow, but each builds on the last until momentum carries it. His claim is that the successful firms had no single miracle moment or big program; results came from many consistent decisions accumulating. The contrast is the doom loop, where a company launches a program, gets weak results, changes direction, and never builds momentum.

The research design: Collins's team screened 1,435 established companies for firms whose cumulative stock returns, after a leap from average results, beat the market by at least three times over fifteen years. They found 11, and compared each with similar companies that did not make the leap. The framework was drawn from the differences.

## When to use it
- Early on, to decide what the business should be about and what it should refuse.
- When the founder is pulled in several directions by opportunities and cannot choose.
- When picking the one number the business is run by.

## Questions to put to the founder
1. What part of this work would you do even when it is not paying yet?
2. What can you do better than most people in your area, and how do you know?
3. What is the one ratio that shows the business is working, for example profit per order or per client?
4. Where do the three answers overlap?
5. What opportunities are you turning down because they sit outside that overlap?
6. What is the repeatable action that will build momentum, and how often will you do it?

## Red flags
- The founder's passion, skill and market do not overlap, but the plan goes ahead anyway.
- The claim to be the best rests on wanting to be, not on evidence.
- A new program or direction every few weeks.
- No single measure of how the engine is running.

## How the pack uses it
founder-board uses the three circles as a lens and writes where they overlap, and where they do not, in founder/board.md. founder-plan includes the single economic ratio as a headline figure in the business plan. founder-ops turns the repeatable action into a routine in founder/ops.md. After launch, founder-metrics tracks that one ratio week by week and flags change of direction.

Example: a founder plans a bakery. Passion: sourdough. Best at: a stable, high-quality loaf, confirmed by tasters. Engine: profit per loaf is healthy on loaves but weak on pastries. The overlap says bake sourdough; the pack suggests dropping the pastry plans and tracking profit per loaf.

## Limits
- The research design is widely criticised. It started from firms that did well and looked backward, so it cannot show that the practices caused the results. Phil Rosenzweig's book The Halo Effect makes this critique in general terms, as summarised by Peter Klein.
- Two 2008 papers in Academy of Management Perspectives tested the 11 firms over later periods. Resnick and Smunt found only one still beating the market on Collins's measure; Niendorf and Beck found the group's long-term returns were not significantly different from the S&P 500 average.
- The firms are large, listed companies; the framework was not built for a cafe or a solo practice.
- Passion is not evidence of demand, so use the circles to focus, not to validate.
