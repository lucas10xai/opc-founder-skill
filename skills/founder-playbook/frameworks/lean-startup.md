# Lean Startup

## The idea
Eric Ries defines a startup as an organisation making a new product or service under extreme uncertainty. There a detailed plan and forecast are unreliable, because no stable history exists to forecast from. His answer is to treat the business as a series of experiments. The book gives five principles; three matter most to a small founder.

Validated learning: the early business exists to learn what a sustainable business looks like, checked by experiment against real customer behaviour, not opinion. Build-Measure-Learn: turn an idea into something customers can react to, measure what they do, then decide whether to pivot (change direction) or persevere, and make that loop fast. Innovation accounting: pick metrics and milestones that show whether you are really learning.

The minimum viable product (MVP) is the version that collects the most validated learning about customers for the least effort. The author says it is not about creating minimal products, needs judgment rather than a formula, and adds overhead because you must actually talk to customers and read the numbers. A small ad pointing to a page describing the offer can be enough to learn that nobody wants it.

Metrics should be actionable, not vanity. Totals like visits or registered users feel good but do not say what to do next. Better are per-customer figures, cohort figures (what share of people who joined in a given week did the next thing) and split tests that confirm or refute a specific guess.

## When to use it
- You have an offer idea and need the cheapest honest test before building.
- You have launched and have numbers but cannot tell whether they show progress.
- You are deciding whether to change the offer, customer or price, or hold course.
- You are tempted to add features because early customers asked.

## Questions to put to the founder
1. What is the single riskiest assumption in your plan?
2. What is the cheapest thing you could do this week to test it with real buyers?
3. What result would convince you the assumption is wrong?
4. What did customers do, not say, in the last test?
5. Of those who tried it in week one, what share came back or paid again?
6. Which number do you track most, and what decision changes if it moves?
7. When will you decide to pivot or persevere, and what will you look at?

## Red flags
- Months of building before anyone pays or pre-orders.
- Success is described only by totals: visits, followers, sign-ups.
- The test has no stated pass or fail threshold.
- Features are added because someone asked, with no test of demand.
- The founder cannot say what the last experiment taught.

## How the pack uses it
founder-launch builds the cheap real test in this form: one assumption, one action, one pass or fail number and a decision date, in founder/launch.md. founder-metrics after launch splits numbers into actionable and vanity and reports cohorts against the plan. founder-plan states the pivot or persevere rule beside the verdict.

Example: a founder wants a meal-prep delivery service. Assumption: office workers will pre-pay for a weekly box. Test: a simple page and 50 invitations in two workplaces, with payment for week one. Pass: at least 8 pay. If fewer pay, interview the non-payers before changing anything.

## Limits
- It grew out of software, where releases are cheap. A restaurant fit-out or lease cannot be iterated in days, so physical businesses should use mock-ups, pop-ups and pre-orders as the experiment.
- The book is mostly stories of companies that worked, and the author warns that success stories suffer from selection bias, so the method is not shown to raise success rates.
- A poor first product can damage a local reputation, so the MVP must still let a customer judge the offer fairly.
- It describes how to learn, not how to find the idea.
