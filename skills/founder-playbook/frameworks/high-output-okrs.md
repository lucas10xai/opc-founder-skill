# High Output Management and OKRs

## The idea
Two related things sit under this name. The first is Andy Grove's view, in High Output Management, that a manager is judged by the output of the people and teams under their care, plus the output of neighbouring teams they influence, not by how busy they are. Grove draws on a factory analogy: define what the process produces, check quality early where mistakes are cheap, and track indicators that show trouble before it reaches the customer. Each indicator is paired with a second one that guards against the side effect of chasing the first, for example speed with error rate. He also talks about managerial leverage: spend time on the few activities that multiply the output of many.

The second is objectives and key results, OKRs. Be precise about origins. Peter Drucker proposed management by objectives in the 1950s. Grove adapted it at Intel, adding the key result as the measure of whether you are getting there, keeping the number of objectives small, and setting them often. John Doerr learned it at Intel, introduced it at Google in the late 1990s, and popularised it in Measure What Matters (2018). Doerr did not invent it, and the version taught today is his telling of Grove's practice.

An objective says where you want to go in plain words. Each key result is a number that shows whether you got there. Grove also recommended stretch: aim high enough that you reach it only about half the time.

## When to use it
- When you are busy every day but cannot say what improved this month.
- When two or three people on a team pull in different directions.
- When the founder needs a monthly or quarterly rhythm of review.
- When a recurring problem needs an early-warning number.

## Questions to put to the founder
- What is the one result that matters most in the next 90 days?
- What number proves it happened, and what is it today?
- What would you stop doing to protect that focus?
- What early signal tells you a month ahead whether you are on track?
- What could that number do badly while looking good, and what second number guards against it?
- Who owns each number, and when do you look at it?
- Did you hit last quarter's targets, and what did you learn from missing?

## Red flags
- More than three objectives for a team of fewer than ten.
- Key results that are tasks ("launch the website") rather than measures.
- Targets nobody can measure with the data they already have.
- Reviewing results only at year end.
- Every target is hit easily, so nothing was stretched.

## How the pack uses it
founder-metrics turns the plan's targets into one objective with three key results and a paired guard number for each, recorded under founder/ after launch. founder-plan checks that the plan's goal has a measurable form. founder-ops uses the early-warning idea when it lists routines.

Example: a small online shop sets the objective "Make repeat buying normal." Key results: repeat customers rise from 10 to 20 percent of orders, average delivery time stays under three days (the guard), and the email list grows by 300. Each is reviewed every Monday.

## Limits
- Written for large knowledge-work organisations; for one or two people, three numbers on a page are enough, and a heavy OKR system is overhead.
- Stretch targets can hurt when they are tied to pay or used as a stick. Grove treated them as one input to review, not a verdict.
- Numbers chosen badly distort behaviour; this is why the pairing matters.
- Much of the popular OKR literature is written by consultants and the software firms that sell it.
