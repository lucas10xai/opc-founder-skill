# Business Model Canvas

## The idea
The canvas is a one-page picture of how a business creates, delivers and earns value from a single business model. It has nine building blocks: customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partnerships and cost structure.

The layout carries an argument. The right side is the market: who the customers are, what you promise them, how you reach them, what kind of relationship you keep, and how they pay. The left side is what it takes to deliver: the resources, activities and partners you need, and what they cost. The value proposition sits in the middle between the two. Because the blocks depend on each other, changing one (say, a different customer segment) forces changes in channels, activities and costs. Strategyzer, which publishes the canvas, advises treating a filled-in canvas as a set of claims: date it, and redraw it as evidence comes in.

Two relatives are worth knowing. The Value Proposition Canvas, from the same group, zooms in on one customer segment: a customer profile (the jobs they are trying to get done, the pains that block them, the gains they want) next to a value map (what you offer, which pains it relieves, which gains it creates). Ash Maurya's Lean Canvas is an adaptation for startups, published in 2010. It keeps five of the nine blocks (customer segments, channels, revenue streams, cost structure and a unique value proposition) and swaps the other four for problem, solution, key metrics and unfair advantage. His stated reason was to put the biggest risks for an unproven startup on the page.

## When to use it
- To describe a whole idea on one page before writing a long plan.
- To compare two possible versions of the business side by side.
- To find which block is weakest or has no evidence yet.
- To explain the business quickly to a partner, adviser or lender.

## Questions to put to the founder
1. Who exactly is your first customer, in one sentence?
2. What is the one thing they get from you that they cannot easily get elsewhere?
3. How will they find you and how will they buy?
4. How do they pay, and how much per sale?
5. Which three activities or resources are essential to deliver the offer?
6. Which suppliers or partners could stop the business if they left?
7. What are your biggest monthly costs?
8. Which block on the canvas is a guess you have not tested?

## Red flags
- More than one customer segment with a different promise for each, all crammed into one canvas.
- A value proposition made of features with no customer problem behind it.
- Revenue streams listed without a price or a count of buyers.
- A canvas with every box filled and no evidence in any of them.
- Channels that the founder has never tried.

## How the pack uses it
founder-plan assembles a canvas from the answers in earlier steps and stores it in the business plan, with an evidence tag (assumed, heard in interviews, tested) on each block. founder-offer takes the value proposition and the customer jobs, pains and gains from founder-interview notes. founder-cfo takes the revenue and cost blocks into founder/cfo.md. founder-launch picks the block with the least evidence as the focus for the first test.

Example: a founder plans a dog-walking service. Segment: working owners in two neighbourhoods. Proposition: reliable midday walks with a photo report. Channels: local group posts and a sign in the pet shop. Relationship: weekly message. Revenue: monthly plans. Costs: insurance, phone, travel time. The least tested block is channels, so the first launch test checks whether the posts bring enquiries.

## Limits
- It is a summary, not an analysis: it says nothing about competition, market size or whether the numbers work.
- Filling in boxes feels like progress even when nothing is validated.
- It describes one model at one moment, and a business with several segments needs several canvases.
- Maurya argues the original misses the biggest startup risks, such as whether the problem is real. Use whichever version makes your riskiest assumptions visible.
