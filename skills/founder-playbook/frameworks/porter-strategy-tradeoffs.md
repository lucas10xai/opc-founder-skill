# Strategy as Trade-offs

## The idea
The 1996 article separates two things people often mix up. Operational effectiveness means doing the same things as your rivals, only better. It matters, but rivals copy it quickly, so it rarely gives lasting advantage on its own. Strategy means choosing to do different activities, or the same activities in a different way, so that you deliver a distinct mix of value. The article's point is that the difference lives in the activities (how you source, serve, sell and deliver), not in a slogan.

Three ideas follow. First, a position: the article describes three starting points, a subset of the products or services in an industry (variety), serving most needs of one group of customers (needs), or reaching customers who are best served in a different way, such as by location or size (access). Second, trade-offs: a position only lasts if it forces you to give something up. An airline that serves meals cannot also turn planes around in minutes; a business that tries both ends up weaker at each. The essence of strategy, the article says, is choosing what not to do. Third, fit: activities should be consistent with the position, reinforce one another, and be coordinated so effort is not wasted. A rival can copy one activity, but a connected set is much harder to copy.

Separate and older idea, labelled clearly: in Competitive Strategy (1980) Porter proposed three generic strategies, cost leadership, differentiation and focus (serving a narrow segment on cost or difference). The 1996 article links its positions back to these and notes that a position can be broad or narrow.

## When to use it
- When the offer sounds like every other business in the category and the founder cannot say what they will do differently.
- When the founder wants to add services or products to please everyone.
- When deciding what to stop doing in a crowded local market.
- When checking that pricing, hours, staffing and channel choices all point the same way.

## Questions to put to the founder
1. Name three activities you will do differently from the nearest competitor.
2. Which customers will you deliberately not serve?
3. What will you refuse to offer, even if a customer asks?
4. Could a competitor copy your edge in a month by adding one service?
5. If you charge a premium, which costs are you accepting; if you charge less, which service are you cutting?
6. Which of your planned activities does not support the main promise?
7. Is your edge based on being cheaper, being different, or serving a narrow group?

## Red flags
- The edge is described only in words like quality, service or passion, with no activity behind it.
- The plan copies a successful competitor feature by feature while keeping the old model.
- The founder tries to be both cheapest and premium.
- The list of things the business will not do is empty.
- Every activity is generic and could be run by anyone.

## How the pack uses it
founder-competitors asks which activities differ from each rival and records them in founder/competitors.md. founder-brand turns the chosen position and the list of things refused into the promise in founder/brand.md. founder-ops checks that suppliers, staffing and routines in founder/ops.md match the position, and founder-board uses the trade-off test as one lens.

Example: a founder opens a bicycle repair shop. Position: same-day repairs for commuters. Trade-offs accepted: no custom builds, no race bikes, closed on weekends. Fit: stock of common parts only, a drop-off rack outside, texted pickup times. A rival that adds same-day repair to a full-service shop would have to give up some of its own services.

## Limits
- Written for large companies with many activities; a two-person business has few activities, so fit shows up as simple consistency rather than a complex system.
- The article's examples are firms that did well, so it says little about how often a distinctive position still fails.
- A small, early business may have to copy the market standard before it can differ, so use the trade-off test to choose a few differences, not to reinvent everything.
- Generic strategies are a coarse sorting device; real positions are more specific.
