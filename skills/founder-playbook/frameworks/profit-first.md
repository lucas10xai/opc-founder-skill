# Profit First

## The idea
The usual formula is sales minus expenses equals profit, which leaves profit as whatever is left over, often nothing. Profit First flips it to sales minus profit equals expenses. The arithmetic is identical, but the behaviour is different: a set share of every deposit is moved to profit before anything is spent, and the business lives on the rest. The author's argument is about human habit, not accounting theory: spending expands to fill the money that is visible.

The mechanism uses separate bank accounts. Michalowicz's own pages and his FAQ describe an income account where all revenue lands, then accounts for profit, owner's pay, tax and operating expenses. Money is divided up on a fixed rhythm, twice a month on the 10th and 25th, by percentages set for each account. Operating expenses get what remains, so the business adjusts spending to fit it.

The book sets target percentages that depend on revenue tier, and he encourages starting with percentages you can actually sustain, then raising the profit share over time. This file does not repeat the tier figures because they change with revenue; use the book's current table. The point to keep is the order: profit and tax first, owner's pay second, expenses last.

## When to use it
- A founder has real revenue but never seems to have cash for tax or for themselves.
- A solo service business mixes personal and business money.
- Margins are thin and costs creep up.
- The founder wants a simple cash habit before they can afford a bookkeeper.
- After the first sales, to set the money rules for month two.

## Questions to put to the founder
- How much did the business bring in last month, and how much of that was profit?
- Do you have a separate bank account for the business?
- How much do you pay yourself, and when?
- How much will you owe in tax this year, and is it set aside?
- Which costs could be cut by a tenth without a customer noticing?
- How many months of fixed costs are sitting in the bank?
- If you moved five percent of every deposit away today, what would you stop paying for?

## Red flags
- Profit is described as "what is left at the end of the year".
- Owner's pay is whatever is in the account.
- Tax is a surprise each year.
- Revenue grows but cash does not.
- One account for everything, personal and business.

## How the pack uses it
founder-cfo adds a cash-allocation section to `founder/cfo.md`: the accounts, the schedule, and a starting split the founder commits to. founder-metrics checks, after launch, whether the transfers were actually made and whether operating expenses fit what is left. founder-plan shows the split in the financial section.

Example (the percentages are invented for illustration, not the book's): a freelance designer takes in 4,000 in a month. Starting split: 5 percent to profit, 15 percent to tax, 40 percent to owner's pay, the rest, 40 percent, to expenses, which is 1,600. If real expenses were 2,200, the gap shows up immediately, before the money is gone, and the founder cuts a cost or lifts prices.

## Limits
- It is a behavioural aid and cash-management habit. It does not replace proper accounts or tax advice.
- It is written for established small businesses with revenue; a pre-revenue founder has nothing to allocate yet.
- Businesses with large cost of goods (a shop with heavy stock) need care so that real revenue, not gross deposits, is allocated; the book addresses this, so read that part.
