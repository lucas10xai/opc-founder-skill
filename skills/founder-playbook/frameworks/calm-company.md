# The Calm Company

## The idea
The Calm Company is a label for the philosophy in two books from 37signals, the software firm behind Basecamp: Rework (2010) and It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work (2018). Both come from the authors' own experience building a small, long-running, profitable business, and both argue against the standard startup script of raising money, hiring fast and growing at any cost.

The business side says: growth is optional, and the right size might be one person, five or forty. Do not use the word "startup" as permission to postpone profit; get revenue and become profitable early, so that no investor timetable decides what you do. Outside funding trades away control. Planning years ahead is mostly guessing, so decide what to do over the next few weeks instead. Constraints help: do less, do it well, say no to most feature and request ideas, and build half a product rather than a half-good one.

The work side says: crazy hours, constant availability and a calendar full of meetings are not signs of seriousness. Protect blocks of uninterrupted time, keep meetings few, and finish what you started before beginning more. Hiring should wait until sustained overload proves it is needed.

## When to use it
- A solo founder or small team deciding whether to raise money or stay self-funded.
- When growth plans quietly assume spending more than the business earns.
- When the founder is working all hours and starting to resent the business.
- When deciding what to leave out of a first offer.
- When setting expectations for the first employee about hours and response times.

## Questions to put to the founder
- Do you need outside money, or only a way to get to the first paying customers?
- In which month does revenue first exceed costs in your plan?
- How many hours a week do you intend to work, and what happens at that limit?
- What is the smallest version of the offer someone would pay for?
- Which three features or services will you refuse to offer?
- What is the first hire, and what overload will have shown it is needed?
- How will customers reach you, and when do you not answer?

## Red flags
- The plan only works if a fundraising round closes.
- The founder expects to work seven days a week "for now" with no end date.
- Large launch costs are paid before anyone has paid for anything.
- A long feature or service list for a business with no customers yet.
- Hiring planned before the founder has done each job personally.

## How the pack uses it
founder-plan and founder-cfo use the profit-early rule to test whether the cash needed in founder/cfo.md can be met without investors. founder-launch uses the "half a product" and uninterrupted-time ideas when it scopes the cheap real test in founder/launch.md. founder-ops sets working hours and hiring triggers in founder/ops.md.

Example: a solo freelance bookkeeper plans to buy software, rent an office and hire an assistant in month one. The framework asks her to sell to three clients first at a real price, work from home, and write a trigger, such as "hire when I turn away two jobs in a month". The trigger goes in founder/ops.md.

## Limits
- It reflects the experience of one company, in software, whose founders could build and sell their own product. Other small firms may have followed similar advice and failed without writing a book about it.
- Staying small does not fit businesses with large fixed costs or a market where scale decides the winner.
- Some of the working-hours advice assumes knowledge work and flexible schedules; a shop, cafe or clinic has opening hours.
- The books are opinion, not research; check the claims against your own numbers.
