# Do Things that Don't Scale

## The idea
The essay argues that a new business rarely takes off by itself. The founders make it take off, and the early push is usually laborious work a big company would never do. The most common form is recruiting the first users one at a time, by hand, instead of waiting for them. The essay treats the early job as getting a foothold in making something people want, and counts the first real users as that foothold.

Concrete tactics follow. Recruit users personally and set them up in person. Make the first users' experience excellent even if the product is incomplete, by being unusually attentive. Start in a deliberately narrow market so a small group feels the offer is for them. Act like a consultant for one customer and shape the offer around that person, as long as you are not billing by the hour. Do by hand what you plan to automate later, so you learn what to build.

It lists starting moves that usually fail: a big launch, and partnerships with large companies. Its argument is that effort, not a splash, gets the first users, and that how happy they are matters more than how many.

It also gives growth arithmetic: ten percent weekly growth turns 100 users into roughly 14,000 after a year. Measure progress by weekly growth rate, not absolute size.

## When to use it
- You have an idea or prototype but few or no paying customers.
- You are tempted to spend on a launch event, ads or a polished site before anyone has bought.
- You think the product is not ready to show.
- You are building a two-sided offer, such as a local marketplace, and need the first participants.

## Questions to put to the founder
1. How many customers have you personally recruited by name in the last 30 days?
2. What did you do for your first customer that you could not do for a thousand?
3. Which narrow group would feel this is made for them?
4. Which part of the service could you deliver by hand this month instead of building a system?
5. How many first customers do you reach each week, and how fast is that number growing?
6. What small gesture would make each early customer feel they chose well?
7. What is your launch-day plan, and what would you do without a launch day?

## Red flags
- The plan relies on a launch, a press mention or a partner for the first customers.
- The founder avoids direct contact with customers and prefers to keep building.
- Early customers are treated as a test sample, not people to delight.
- No count of customers the founder personally brought in.
- Waiting for the product to be finished before showing anyone.

## How the pack uses it
founder-launch applies it to the cheap real test and the first 30 days: the plan must name how the first customers are recruited personally and which manual steps replace automation, recorded in founder/launch.md. founder-marketing checks that the 30-day campaign puts direct recruiting before paid reach, in founder/marketing.md. founder-board uses it as a lens: is there a believable hand-recruiting path?

Example: an online shop selling home-baked cakes plans to launch with ads. The framework redirects it: message 30 people in two local parent groups, deliver the first 20 orders in person with a handwritten note, and log what each buyer asks for. The launch file then tracks weekly repeat buyers, not site visits.

## Limits
- Written for software startups chasing fast growth. For a cafe or local service, many "unscalable" acts are simply the business and never stop.
- Hand work done for pay can turn the venture into a consultancy by accident.
- The examples are survivors, so the tactic says little about the odds of working.
- A narrow starting market only helps if a bigger market sits behind it.
