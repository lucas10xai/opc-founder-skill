# Jobs to Be Done

## The idea
Jobs to Be Done is a way of looking at why people buy. Instead of describing the buyer (age, income, habits) or the product (features), it asks what progress the buyer is trying to make in a particular situation. Buying is described as hiring a product to do a job. If it does the job well, the buyer hires it again; if not, they drop it. A job has functional, social and emotional sides, and the circumstances matter more than the buyer's profile.

The best-known story is the milkshake. A fast-food chain improved its milkshakes according to surveys and sales barely moved. Christensen and colleagues, including Bob Moesta, studied when and why people bought them. Many bought one in the morning, alone, to keep them occupied and not hungry on a dull commute; the rivals were bananas, bagels and doughnuts. At other times a parent bought one as a treat for a child. The details come from talks by those involved, not published data, so treat it as an illustration.

Who contributed what, as far as sources show: Christensen developed the idea and wrote it up with Hall, Dillon and Duncan in Harvard Business Review (2016) and in the book Competing Against Luck (2016). Moesta, with Chris Spiek at the Re-Wired Group, developed the interview practice. Moesta's condominium project is a documented case: buyers hesitated over what they would give up by moving, and sales grew when the offer helped with the move.

The interview practice follows the buyer's real story of a purchase, from first thought through looking around to the decision. It looks at four forces: the push of the current situation, the pull of a new option, anxiety about the new option, and habit of the current way. Moesta's rule of thumb is that without some push, even a buyer who likes your product will not switch. That is a practitioner heuristic, not a tested law.

## When to use it
- You cannot say who the real competitor is.
- Surveys say customers like the product but sales do not follow.
- You are choosing which customer moment to serve in positioning or an offer.
- You are interviewing buyers about a recent purchase or switch.

## Questions to put to the founder
1. What was happening in your customer's day just before they bought?
2. What did they use or do before you, and what did they stop?
3. What did they try and abandon first?
4. What nearly stopped them buying?
5. Do customers buy for more than one reason, in different situations?
6. What do you think you sell, and what do customers say it is for?
7. What would a customer do if you did not exist?

## Red flags
- Customers are described only by age, income or interests.
- Only identical products are named as competitors.
- Features praised in a survey were improved and sales did not change.
- The founder cannot describe one real purchase from first thought to payment.

## How the pack uses it
founder-interview uses the switch-story format: each buyer walks through a recent purchase, and the push, pull, anxiety and habit are recorded in founder/interviews/. founder-competitors adds "what they did before" so non-obvious rivals appear in founder/competitors.md. founder-offer turns the anxieties into objections the offer must answer, in founder/offer.md.

Example: a founder opens a small bakery near a train station. Commuters say they buy a pastry for the platform because they skipped breakfast. The competitor is the vending machine and the sandwich shop, so the offer stresses speed and carry-friendly packaging.

## Limits
- The milkshake and many cases are told by the method's developers, with few independent figures, and reported cases are the successes.
- Several authors use the term differently. This file follows the Christensen and Moesta line.
- A few interviews show a pattern, not its size.
- It explains a purchase that happened; it does not set prices or predict volume.
