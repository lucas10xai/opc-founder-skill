# Positioning

## The idea
Ries and Trout argue that positioning is not something you do to a product; it is something you do to the mind of the buyer. People are bombarded with messages, so they filter most of them out and keep only what fits what they already believe. Your job is to find a simple, believable place in that crowded mind and occupy it. The authors first set out the idea in a 1972 series of articles in Advertising Age and expanded it in the 1981 book.

Several practical rules come out of this. First, being first is the easiest way in: people remember who came first in a category and tend to forget the second. If you cannot be first in an existing category, make a new one you can be first in. Second, buyers sort brands onto a mental ladder, roughly one rung each, so ask which rung you can realistically hold. Third, a follower should not attack the leader head on; the authors recommend finding a gap the leader does not hold and defining yourself against the leader. Their examples include a rental firm that built its message around being number two and a soft drink that called itself the alternative to cola. Fourth, the name and the message should be simple and tied to the benefit, and a brand stretched across several unlike products loses its meaning.

The cost of a muddled message is the other half of the argument. If you try to say five things, buyers retain none, and a business that means different things to different people owns no place in anyone's head.

## When to use it
- When choosing the one sentence that defines the business for a new customer.
- When a competitor is much better known and the founder must decide how to stand against them.
- When the name, sign or website has to say what the business is in seconds.
- When tempted to add unrelated products to the same brand.

## Questions to put to the founder
1. Finish the sentence: we are the best choice for [who] who want [what].
2. What category will a new customer put you in, and who is first in it?
3. Who is the best-known competitor, and what are they known for?
4. What are you known for that the leader is not?
5. Can a stranger repeat your message after hearing it once?
6. What word could you own in your area, such as fastest, cheapest or most specialist?
7. What would you stop saying to keep the message clear?

## Red flags
- The message lists more than one benefit and cannot be shortened.
- The founder says the business is for everyone.
- The positioning copies the leader's words.
- The name says nothing about what the business does and the budget for explaining it is zero.
- The brand is being used for several unlike products.

## How the pack uses it
founder-competitors records what each rival is known for in founder/competitors.md. founder-brand takes the one-sentence position, the category and the leader contrast and writes them into the promise and name in founder/brand.md. founder-marketing builds the 30-day campaign around that single idea in founder/marketing.md. founder-board checks that the stated position matches what customers said.

Example: a founder opens a tutoring service. A national chain leads on convenience. The founder's position: the specialist for maths exam resits in one town, with the leader contrast being generalist and multi-subject. The sign, website and every ad say that one line, and the founder declines English tutoring enquiries for the first year.

## Limits
- The book's examples are large brands from the era of mass advertising; a small business may reach people through word of mouth and local search, where the rules work differently.
- The recommendations are argued by cases, not tested statistically, so treat them as strong heuristics.
- Owning a narrow place can cap growth; the authors accept that trade.
- Being first in a category you invented does not help if few buyers care about the category.
- Positioning must match what the business really delivers, or buyers find out.
