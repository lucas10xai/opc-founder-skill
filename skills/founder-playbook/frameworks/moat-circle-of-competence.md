# Circle of Competence, Moat and Margin of Safety

## The idea
Three ideas from the value investing tradition, each useful on its own.

Circle of competence: an investor does not need to understand everything, only the businesses inside a circle he can honestly evaluate. The size of the circle matters little. Knowing where its edge lies matters a great deal. Buffett wrote this in the 1996 letter.

Moat: in the 1995 letter Buffett says he looks for businesses that are like a castle protected by a moat that rivals cannot cross, and he describes GEICO's moat as widening. A moat is whatever lets a business keep earning good returns when competitors arrive. The 1996 letter ties GEICO's moat to economies of scale in costs.

Margin of safety: this is Benjamin Graham's idea, which Buffett credits in the 1997 letter, where he says high prices had eroded it. Do not pay a price at which you need everything to go right. Leave room for estimating errors and bad luck. Graham set it out in The Intelligent Investor (1949, many later editions). Charlie Munger, Buffett's long-time partner, worked in the same tradition, and his talks are collected in Poor Charlie's Almanack (2005).

For a small business the same questions apply to the founder rather than a share price: do you know this field, what stops a copycat, and how wrong can your numbers be before you are broke.

## When to use it
- Choosing between several business ideas and one sits close to the founder's real experience.
- Judging whether a profitable idea will stay profitable once someone copies it.
- Setting how much cash and how many months of runway to hold against bad estimates.
- Deciding whether to expand into an unfamiliar product line.
- Reading a competitor map and asking which rivals could hurt you.

## Questions to put to the founder
- How many years have you worked or bought in this field?
- Which parts of the business do you know cold, and which are guesses?
- If a bigger or cheaper rival opened next door, what would customers still come to you for?
- Which of these do you have: a loyal local base, a hard-to-copy supplier deal, a lower cost per unit, a location, a reputation?
- How long would it take a competitor to copy the thing that makes you different?
- If sales come in 30 percent below your plan, how many months can you last?
- Which single assumption, if wrong, ends the business?

## Red flags
- The founder cannot name an edge except hard work or passion.
- The plan only works if the best-case sales happen.
- The idea sits outside the founder's experience and no partner covers the gap.
- The edge is a product feature that a rival can copy in a week.
- Margins are thin and any cost rise would wipe them out.

## How the pack uses it
founder-board runs a competence lens and a moat lens and writes the verdict in `founder/board.md`. founder-competitors asks for each rival's moat and records it in `founder/competitors.md`. founder-cfo applies the margin of safety by stress-testing sales downward and writing the cash buffer in `founder/cfo.md`.

Example: a founder plans a second-hand bike shop. Competence: eight years as a mechanic. Small-scale moat: repair work done by a trusted local, repeat customers, and a steady supply of trade-ins. No scale moat against online stores. Margin of safety: the plan must survive a quiet winter at 70 percent of forecast sales.

## Limits
- Written about buying shares in large, listed companies; a small shop rarely has a durable moat in the investing sense. The honest answer is often a thin moat, which is fine if the founder is cheap and good.
- A circle of competence can become an excuse never to learn or expand.
- Buffett and Munger are survivors of a long, successful record, so the framework may overstate how reliably anyone can judge moats in advance.
- The margin of safety has no fixed number; it is a judgement.
