# Two-Way Doors, 70 Percent Information and Regret Minimization

## The idea
Three separate ideas from Jeff Bezos are often bundled together, so the pack keeps them apart.

First, the door idea comes from the Amazon shareholder letter for 2015 (published in 2016). Some decisions are consequential and cannot be undone, or nearly cannot. The letter calls these one-way doors, or Type 1 decisions, and says they deserve slow, careful, consulted decision making. Most decisions can be reversed: two-way doors, or Type 2. These should be made quickly by a small number of people, because being wrong costs little. The warning is that a large organisation tends to treat every decision like a Type 1.

Second, the 2016 letter (published in 2017) adds that most decisions should probably be made with somewhere around 70 percent of the information you wish you had. Waiting for 90 percent is usually just being slow. The letter ties this to being quick at noticing and correcting bad decisions, so that being wrong is cheaper than being slow.

Third, regret minimization is not from the letters. Bezos described it in an interview for the Academy of Achievement (class of 2001) when explaining his decision to leave a stable job and start an online bookshop. He imagined himself at age 80 looking back, and asked which choice he would regret. He concluded he would not regret trying and failing, but might regret never trying. It is a tie-breaker for big life choices, not a method for operations.

## When to use it
- You are stuck deciding and cannot tell whether the choice is big or small.
- You are about to sign a long lease, buy expensive equipment or quit a job.
- You are spending a week researching something you could simply try for a month.
- You need a rule for which decisions you can delegate to staff.
- The risk of the whole venture feels paralysing and you need a personal long-term test.

## Questions to put to the founder
- If this turns out wrong, what does it cost to undo, in money and weeks?
- Is it a one-way door: a contract, a loan, stock you cannot return, a public commitment?
- What information are you waiting for, and when would you know it?
- Can you get that information with a small test instead of more research?
- Who has to be consulted if it is a one-way door, and who does not if it is not?
- If you are looking back in many years, which choice would you regret more?
- What is the smallest version of this that you can reverse?

## Red flags
- A founder treats a two-week trial with the same care as signing a lease.
- A founder commits to a long contract on a Tuesday because a supplier said the price ends Friday.
- Months of research with no experiment.
- Regret is used to justify a decision with no cash buffer behind it.
- Every decision waits for the founder, even trivial ones.

## How the pack uses it
founder-board classifies each pending decision as one-way or two-way and records it in `founder/board.md`. founder-launch prefers reversible first steps (a pop-up, a pre-order, a month-to-month space) and puts the irreversible ones after the test in `founder/launch.md`. founder-cfo marks which spending is hard to recover.

Example: a founder wants a three-year shop lease and a coffee machine. The lease is a one-way door: it needs a break clause, a cash check and a second opinion. A first batch of menu items is a two-way door: try it for two weeks, then change it.

## Limits
- The letters are management essays by a company with huge resources; a small founder has less cushion, so more decisions are one-way than they seem. A personal guarantee on a loan is one example.
- Seventy percent is a rough guide, not a measured threshold.
- Regret minimization can justify risk the founder cannot afford; it should come after the numbers, not instead of them.
- The accounts are self-reported by a winner.
