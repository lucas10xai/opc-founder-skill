# First Principles Reasoning

## The idea
In Aristotle's philosophy, a first principle (in Greek, archê) is a starting point that other knowledge is built from. Reasoning from first principles means going back to what is basic and known to be true and building the conclusion from there, rather than starting from habit or what other people already do. Aristotle's own use of the term concerns science and philosophy, not business; the business use is a later borrowing.

The business version that is well documented comes from a 2012 Wired interview of Elon Musk by Chris Anderson. Musk said he approaches problems with a physics mindset, reasoning from first principles rather than by analogy. His example was rockets: he asked what a rocket is made of, what those materials cost on the commodity market, and concluded that materials were a small share of the usual price, so there must be inefficiencies in how rockets were made and bought. That is a statement in the interview; the figures are his, not independently checked here.

What is popular retelling: that the method is a special trick, that Musk invented it, or that it always yields a cheaper product. The interview shows only the approach and his own claim that it pointed to a gap. Whether it worked out is a separate question.

## When to use it
- Pricing: you are told "everyone charges this" and want to know why.
- Costing: a supplier quote or a rule of thumb seems too high.
- Spotting a gap between the cost of the parts and the price of the whole.
- Checking an assumption in the plan that has no evidence behind it.
- Deciding whether to copy a competitor's model.

## Questions to put to the founder
- What is the product made of, and what does each part cost?
- What labour hours does one unit take, at what hourly cost?
- What fixed costs does each unit have to carry?
- What do customers pay for the finished product, and for what reason?
- Which cost in your plan comes from a rule of thumb rather than a quote or a measurement?
- What does the best-run competitor pay for the same inputs?
- What would make the whole thing cheaper that nobody does today?

## Red flags
- A key number was copied from a blog or "industry average" and never checked.
- The founder cannot list the parts of the cost of one unit.
- Price is set only by copying a competitor.
- The plan's savings depend on an assumption nobody has tested.
- A big gap between parts cost and price is read as an opportunity without asking what the gap pays for (labour, rent, service, risk).

## How the pack uses it
founder-pricing and founder-cfo use the procedure below to build the unit economics in founder/cfo.md and the price range in founder/pricing.md. founder-competitors uses it to ask why competitors charge what they do.

Procedure: (1) write the product as parts: materials, labour, space, tools, fees and your own time. (2) Put a measured or quoted cost on each part. (3) Add the costs to get the true cost of one unit. (4) Compare it with what the market charges and with what customers say they would pay. (5) For every large gap, name what the gap pays for. (6) Mark each number as checked or assumed, and check the assumed ones that matter most.

Example: a founder selling hand-poured candles at 18 finds materials are 3 and labour is 4. The gap to 18 covers packaging, fees, marketing and her time, which she had not counted. The check reveals margin is thinner than she expected.

## Limits
- The method fails when the basic facts are wrong. A bad supplier quote or a missed cost at the bottom produces a confident wrong answer.
- Parts cost is not the same as the cost of a business. Rent, risk, service, brand and time are real costs the formula can leave out.
- It is slow, so use it on the few decisions that matter, not on everything.
- Survivorship bias: we hear of the cases where the reasoning led to a success.
