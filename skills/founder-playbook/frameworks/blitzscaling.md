# Blitzscaling

## The idea
Hoffman and Yeh define blitzscaling as prioritising speed over efficiency while the outcome is still uncertain. The authors separate it from other kinds of fast growth. Classic startup growth stays efficient while you learn what the market wants. Fastscaling spends inefficiently to grow quicker, but in a market where the costs are well understood. Blitzscaling accepts large inefficiency and mistakes, without waiting for certainty, because being slow is judged to be the bigger risk.

The logic is first-scaler advantage. In some markets, the company that gets big first collects compounding benefits, for example network effects, better recruiting and easier access to capital. If the prize is big and rivals are racing for it, the authors say being efficient is irrelevant if you lose. They describe a sequence: establish product-market fit efficiently, then blitzscale, then relax into efficient growth once leadership is secure. They also say it needs large amounts of capital and talent, and that it carries serious risk of collapse.

For a small-business founder the useful part is the gate. Blitzscaling only makes sense when winning the market, and winning it quickly, is worth more than anything you give up. Almost no cafe, shop, agency or solo service meets that test.

## When to use it
- Mostly as a check that you should not do it.
- Software or platform ideas where each new user makes the product better for the others.
- Markets where several funded rivals are already racing and the winner takes most of the value.
- When an investor is asking for hypergrowth, so you can judge whether the market justifies it.

## Questions to put to the founder
- Does each new customer make your product more valuable to the next one?
- If a competitor got to a big scale first, could you still earn a living? Yes or no.
- Do customers or suppliers switch easily, or are they locked in?
- Have you already proved that customers want this and pay for it?
- How much money would growing three times faster need, and who provides it?
- What happens to your quality if you double staff in six months?
- Could you live comfortably as the second or third biggest in the market?

## Red flags
- Calling a local, repeatable business "a platform" to justify burning cash.
- Growth plan funded by loans against personal assets.
- Spending on marketing before any proof customers return.
- Quality or service dropping as volume increases.
- No clear definition of who the one winner in the market would be.

## How the pack uses it
founder-board runs the "winner-take-most" check as one lens and records the answer in founder/board.md. founder-plan then marks the growth mode as efficient growth or fast growth, and founder-cfo shows the cash cost if the founder wants speed. founder-pitch uses it only when an investor audience expects scale.

Example: a founder opening a neighbourhood coffee cart gets the "do I need first-scaler advantage" question. Answer: no, each customer is local, switching is easy and a second cart is just another cart. The board records "efficient growth", and the plan funds growth from profit.

## Limits
- Written by a venture investor and a co-author; the examples are mostly venture-backed software firms that won, which is survivorship bias.
- Scholars have examined the idea; for example, a 2020 Business Horizons article by Kuratko, Holt and Neubert looks at what blitzscaling is and how it is used in practice.
- It assumes large outside capital. A bootstrapped founder usually cannot afford its inefficiency.
- Most small businesses compete on quality, location and trust, not on being first to scale.
- Rapid scaling can damage staff, service and finances; the authors themselves warn of this.
