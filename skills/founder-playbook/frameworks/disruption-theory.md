# Disruption Theory

## The idea
Christensen began by asking why successful, well-run companies sometimes lose their position. His answer is that doing the sensible things, listening to your best customers and investing in better products for them, can blind a firm to a different kind of challenger. He distinguishes two kinds of innovation. Sustaining innovation improves products for the leading firms' best, most profitable customers, and incumbents are generally good at it. Disruptive innovation starts with an offer that is simpler, cheaper or more convenient and, by the standards the incumbents care about, worse.

The theory then names two entry routes. Low-end disruption enters at the bottom of an existing market with a good enough product at a lower price, aimed at customers who are being given more than they need. Incumbents tend to retreat upmarket to higher margins rather than fight for those customers. New-market disruption targets people who were not buying at all, serving an underserved group or creating a new segment. In both cases the challenger improves over time and moves up until it competes for the incumbent's main customers.

The theory comes from close study of one industry, hard disk drives, in the 1970s and 1980s. Christensen and his co-authors' 2015 article insists the label be used narrowly: the classic pattern is a small entrant that serves customers the leaders overlook with a modest offer, then climbs toward the leaders' core buyers. They say many firms called disruptors, such as Uber, do not fit it.

## When to use it
- As a threat check: when a cheaper, simpler alternative is gaining ground among your least demanding customers.
- As an entry check: when you want to serve people the established firms overserve or ignore.
- When deciding whether to defend a premium product or follow customers downmarket.

## Questions to put to the founder
1. Are the established firms giving most customers more than they need, and how do you know?
2. Which customers are ignored or priced out today?
3. What would a good-enough, cheaper version look like, and would anyone buy it?
4. Why would the established firms not copy you, in terms of their margins or model?
5. How will your offer improve over the next three years to reach better customers?

## Red flags
- The founder calls the business disruptive but offers a better product at a higher price to the same customers.
- No incumbent is overserving anyone; customers are still asking for more.
- The plan says larger firms will not respond, with no reason given.

## How the pack uses it
founder-competitors asks, for each larger rival, whether the founder is competing at its low end, in a new market, or head to head, and records the answer in founder/competitors.md. founder-board treats the claim of disruption as a hypothesis, and its lens marks it unproven unless the overserved-customer evidence is present in founder/board.md. founder-plan notes any simpler or cheaper substitute that could attack the founder's own premium offer.

Example: a founder plans a one-hour phone repair kiosk. Established repair shops quote two days and a premium price. If most customers say they would take a basic screen repair at a lower price, the kiosk is a low-end entry. If instead most customers want a better quality repair, the kiosk is simply a competitor and should be treated as one.

## Limits
- A study of 77 cases by Andrew King and Baljir Baatartogtokh found, by the judgement of industry experts, that only 7 (9 percent) showed all four elements they identified in the theory; they conclude it works as a warning, not a prediction.
- Jill Lepore's 2014 New Yorker essay argues that disruption is an account of why some businesses fail, not a general explanation of change.
- Most small businesses compete head to head with local rivals; calling that disruption misleads.
