# Customer Development

## The idea
Steve Blank's starting point is that a new business needs a process for finding customers that runs alongside the process of making the product. Most failures, he argues, come from building and selling as if customers and market were already known. The model has four steps: customer discovery, customer validation, customer creation and company building.

Discovery is done by the founders, outside the building: talking to potential customers to test the plan's hypotheses. The book names three: that the problem you believe you solve matters to them, that your product solves it, and who exactly the customer is, including who decides to buy. It says the aim is not to collect feature lists or run focus groups. The founders' vision defines the first product; the job is to learn whether customers and a market exist for it.

Validation turns that into a repeatable way of selling, proved by getting customers to pay. A purchase counts for more than polite words. If too few customers pay, the model sends you back to discovery. Only when repeatable customers and a repeatable sales process add up to a profitable model do you move on. Customer creation then builds demand, and company building turns a learning team into a department-based organisation.

Two more ideas matter. Each step is a loop, and going back is normal learning, not failure. And spending stays low until paying customers are found, because extra cash early can hide whether a market exists. The model also separates market types (existing, resegmented, new), which change what sales and marketing should do.

## When to use it
- You have an idea but have not spoken to people who would pay for it.
- The founder has built for months and cannot name five buyers.
- You sell to businesses or a few big buyers and need to know who decides.
- You are about to hire sales staff or buy marketing before a sale process is proven.

## Questions to put to the founder
1. Which problem does your customer say is costliest or most annoying, in their words?
2. How many potential customers have you spoken to in person this month?
3. Who pays, who decides and who uses it, and are they the same person?
4. How many people have paid or committed money?
5. Can you describe, step by step, how the last three sales happened?
6. Which assumption in your plan have customers contradicted?
7. How much have you spent on sales and marketing before the first repeat sale?

## Red flags
- No conversations with buyers beyond friends and family.
- The founder starts from a feature list and asks buyers to choose.
- Marketing spend or hires planned before any sale is repeatable.
- The "customer" is a broad group with no named first buyers.
- Failure to find buyers is explained away instead of sending the founder back to discovery.

## How the pack uses it
founder-interview applies discovery: it states the three hypotheses (problem, product, customer) before the 5 to 10 real conversations and records which were confirmed or contradicted in founder/interviews/. founder-sales uses validation for business buyers: who decides, a tested first outreach, and whether orders follow. founder-launch treats a paid first sale as the exit from validation. founder-plan flags spending that happens before validation.

Example: a founder offers bookkeeping for small tradesmen. Hypotheses: they lose evenings to paperwork, will pay monthly, and the owner decides. After 8 conversations, 6 say the pain is real but 5 say a spouse handles it. The customer hypothesis is revised before any marketing is bought.

## Limits
- Written for technology startups and mainly business sales. A cafe or shop can often test directly, with first customers visible in days.
- It gives checkpoints, not numbers for what counts as enough.
- The book's case rests largely on contrasting one success with one failure in one market, which is weak evidence that the process causes the difference.
- Customer talks still need good technique, or polite answers pass as validation (see the Mom Test).
