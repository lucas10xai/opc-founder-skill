# Walton's Ten Rules for Building a Business

## The idea
In his autobiography, the founder of Walmart lists ten rules that he says worked for him, and he says they are not commandments. They are a practical description of running a store-based business with many staff and many customers. Paraphrased, they are:

1. Commit to the business and care about it more than anyone else.
2. Share profits with the people who work with you and treat them as partners.
3. Keep people motivated with high goals, friendly competition and scorekeeping, and avoid being predictable.
4. Tell staff as much about the business as you can, so they care.
5. Thank people sincerely for what they do.
6. Celebrate success and keep the mood light.
7. Listen to everyone and build ways to get them talking, so responsibility moves down and ideas move up.
8. Give customers what they want and a little more; own mistakes, apologise and stand behind your work.
9. Keep expenses lower than competitors, because efficiency lets you survive mistakes.
10. Go against the crowd; if everyone does it one way, a niche may exist in the opposite direction.

The rules fall into three groups: people (2 to 7), customers (8), and money and positioning (9 and 10). Rule 1 is the base for all of them.

## When to use it
- A shop, cafe, salon or any business where staff meet customers every day.
- When hiring the first few people and deciding how to treat and pay them.
- When cost is creeping up and nobody is watching it.
- When customers complain and staff do not feel able to fix things.
- When trying to find a niche against bigger competitors.

## Questions to put to the founder
- How will you share the business's results with your staff, in money or information?
- What do staff tell you that you did not already know? How often do you ask?
- What do you do when a customer complaint is the business's fault?
- What is the "little extra" you give each customer, and what does it cost per customer?
- Which three costs are your biggest, and what do competitors pay for the same?
- What are your competitors all doing that you could do differently?
- When did you last thank someone, specifically, for something?

## Red flags
- The founder decides everything and staff are not asked for input.
- Complaints are argued with, not fixed.
- Costs are reviewed only when cash runs short.
- Pay is the lowest in the area and turnover is high.
- Positioning copies the nearest competitor.

## How the pack uses it
founder-ops uses rules 2 to 7 to shape the staffing and routines section of founder/ops.md. founder-cfo uses rule 9 to compare the cost lines in founder/cfo.md with local benchmarks. founder-brand uses rule 8 when writing the promise in founder/brand.md, and founder-metrics checks the customer and expense measures after launch.

Example: a founder of a small bakery with four staff plans a weekly ten-minute chat where staff report what customers asked for, a monthly look at the three biggest costs, and a standing rule that any staff member can replace a wrong order free. The routine and the rule go in founder/ops.md.

## Limits
- The rules are a founder's own account of his own success, written later, so they carry hindsight and survivorship bias.
- Walmart's scale and labour practices have been criticised, for example for low wages; Barbara Ehrenreich's Nickel and Dimed (2001) is one well-known account. The rule on sharing profits is not always matched by practice at large retailers.
- The rules are principles, not a method; they do not tell you how much to pay or what to price.
- The "lowest cost" approach works for volume retail and can hurt a business competing on craft or service.
