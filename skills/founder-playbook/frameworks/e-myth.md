# The E-Myth

## The idea
Gerber's starting claim is that most small businesses are not started by entrepreneurs at all. They are started by skilled workers, a baker, a plumber, a designer, who get tired of working for someone else and open their own place. The mistake he calls the fatal assumption: believing that knowing the craft means you know how to run a business that sells the craft. Those are different jobs.

The book splits every owner into three personalities. The technician wants to do the work. The manager wants order, routine and predictability. The entrepreneur wants to spot opportunities and change things. Most founders are mostly technician, so the manager and entrepreneur work in the business goes undone, and the owner ends up with a job that has no boss and no days off.

The remedy is to work on the business, not only in it. You treat the business itself as the product: design how every repeatable task is done, write it down, train people to follow it, and measure the result. Gerber frames this as building a prototype that could be copied many times, the way a franchise is. The point for a small founder is not to become a franchisor but to get the business out of your head and into documented routines, so it can run without you in every step.

## When to use it
- A one-person or few-person service or shop where the owner is the bottleneck for everything.
- Before hiring the first employee: what exactly would you hand over, and where is it written down?
- When quality varies from day to day or person to person.
- When the founder feels trapped by the business and cannot take a week off.
- When planning to open a second location or sell the business.

## Questions to put to the founder
- Which of your tasks could a new hire do on day three if you gave them written steps?
- How many hours a week do you spend on the craft itself versus on selling, finance and organising?
- If you were unavailable for two weeks, what would stop?
- Which three routines (opening, ordering, handling a complaint) are not written anywhere?
- Which of technician, manager or entrepreneur are you least interested in, and who covers it?
- How do you check that a job was done to your standard without redoing it?
- What number tells you each week that the business is healthy?

## Red flags
- The owner does all the selling, all the delivery and all the admin personally.
- Every answer to "how do you do X" starts with "it depends, I just know".
- Growth plan is "hire people like me".
- No weekly time set aside for work on the business.
- The founder cannot say what the business would be worth to a buyer who is not them.

## How the pack uses it
founder-ops uses the three personalities as a quick self-check and lists the routines to document in the staffing and routines section of founder/ops.md. founder-plan flags a plan whose entire operation depends on the founder's hands, and founder-cfo adds the cost of the time the founder cannot yet delegate.

Example: a founder plans a home-cleaning service and will do every job. The framework asks for a one-page checklist per job type, a pricing sheet and a photo standard for "done", so that a second cleaner can be added without the owner re-inspecting each home. The routines list lands in founder/ops.md, and the cost of the second cleaner's training time is added to founder/cfo.md.

## Limits
- It was written for owner-operated craft businesses. A pure solo consultant may rightly choose to stay a technician.
- Written systems help repeatable work. Highly bespoke work resists a full manual, and over-documenting can slow a tiny business.
- The franchise-prototype advice suits businesses meant to be copied; most founders will not need it.
- Gerber's company sells coaching built on the book, so treat failure statistics quoted in its materials with care. Check them against public data such as the US Bureau of Labor Statistics survival tables, which count establishments, not founders.
