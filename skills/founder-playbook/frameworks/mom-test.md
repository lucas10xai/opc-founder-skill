# The Mom Test

## The idea
People are polite. Tell a friend, relative or stranger your idea and ask if it is good, and they will usually say yes to spare your feelings. The Mom Test is a standard for questions: a good question would draw a truthful, useful answer even from your own mother. The founder, not the customer, must avoid the lie, by choosing questions that leave no room for flattery.

The book boils this into three rules. Talk about the person's life, not your idea. Ask about specific things that happened in the past, not generic habits or opinions about the future. Talk less and listen more. "Would you buy this?" invites a guess. A question about the last time they faced the problem and what they did returns a fact.

It names three kinds of bad data: compliments (free to give, so worthless), fluff (generic claims, future promises and hypothetical maybes) and feature ideas taken at face value (understand why someone wants a feature, do not just build it). Deflect compliments and steer back to facts.

A good conversation is measured by what the other person gave up. Fitzpatrick's terms are commitment and advancement. Commitment means they spend something real: time (a follow-up meeting, a trial), reputation (an introduction to a boss or peer, a public testimonial) or money (a pre-order, deposit or letter of intent). Advancement means they move a step along the real path toward buying. A meeting that ends with no next step did not go well, however pleasant. A refused request is still information.

## When to use it
- You are about to talk to potential customers for the first time.
- Friends and family love the idea and you do not know what that means.
- Interview notes are full of praise but nobody has paid or booked.
- You are choosing a first customer group and signals are mixed.

## Questions to put to the founder
1. When did you last speak to a possible customer, and what did they say they actually did?
2. How many people have given you money, a booking or a named introduction?
3. What does the problem cost the customer now, in time or money?
4. What have they already tried to fix it?
5. What did you ask for at the end of the last conversation, and what was the answer?
6. Who else did they suggest you talk to?
7. How many of your notes are compliments or "I would" statements?

## Red flags
- The founder describes the idea at the start of every conversation.
- Evidence is "great idea", "I would definitely use it" or "let me know when it's ready".
- No conversation ended with a clear next step.
- Ten customers describe ten different problems.

## How the pack uses it
founder-interview applies the three rules to its conversation guide and tags every note as fact, compliment, fluff or idea, ending each conversation with a recorded commitment level (none, time, reputation, money) in founder/interviews/. founder-sales judges outreach by advancement: the funnel step reached counts more than warm replies. founder-pricing treats pre-orders as stronger evidence than stated willingness to pay. founder-launch uses a money commitment as the first test's pass mark.

Example: a founder plans a dog-walking service in an apartment block. Instead of "Would you pay 15 for a walk?", the founder asks about the last week the dog could not get out. One neighbour has paid a student twice. The founder ends by offering a 4-week trial at a stated price; two agree and one pays in advance.

## Limits
- It is one practitioner's handbook, not a researched method, so its rules are reasoned and anecdotal.
- As summarised by a reviewer, the book says conversations cannot show whether you can build the product, only whether customers care.
- The same reviewer notes a tension between not pitching and asking for commitment, since customers cannot commit to an offer never described.
- A few conversations show direction, not market size. Keep going until you stop learning, and narrow the customer group if answers scatter.
