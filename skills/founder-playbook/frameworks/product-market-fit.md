# Product/Market Fit and the Sean Ellis Test

## The idea
Marc Andreessen's 2007 essay argues that of a startup's three big ingredients, team, product and market, the market matters most. In a strong market, customers pull the product out of the company: the first workable product will be bought, and it need not be great. In a poor market, a great team and product cannot rescue the business. He credits Andy Rachleff with the formulation that lack of market is the number one company killer.

His main instruction follows. Product/market fit means being in a good market with a product that can satisfy it. Before you have it, focus on getting it and do whatever is required; almost everything else can wait. He says founders can feel which side they are on. Without fit, customers are not quite getting value, word of mouth is not spreading, usage is not growing fast, and deals do not close. With fit, customers buy as fast as you can supply them. These are his observations from experience, not measurements.

The Sean Ellis test tries to turn the feeling into a number. Ask users how they would feel if they could no longer use the product: very disappointed, somewhat disappointed or not disappointed. In a 2009 interview Ellis said that above 40 percent very disappointed he tends to say a product has found fit, and below it, not. First Round Review reports he got there by benchmarking about a hundred startups. Ellis suggested surveying people who have come back at least once, with about 30 responses as a minimum.

## When to use it
- You are deciding whether to keep improving the offer or start spending on growth.
- You have a few dozen customers and want one honest read on how much they would miss you.
- You feel busy and cannot tell if the business is pulling or being pushed.

## Questions to put to the founder
1. If you stopped trading tomorrow, how many customers would ask why?
2. What share of customers come back without prompting?
3. How many new customers came last month by recommendation?
4. Do customers buy as fast as you can serve them, or do you chase each sale?
5. Among repeat customers, what share would be very disappointed to lose you? (Ask at least 30.)
6. What do those very disappointed people have in common?

## Red flags
- Growth depends on constant founder push, discounts or paid reach.
- Few customers return and fewer recommend.
- Under 40 percent say they would be very disappointed.
- Lack of demand is blamed on the team or marketing without checking the market.

## How the pack uses it
founder-metrics runs the Ellis question on real customers after launch, among those who have bought at least twice, and reports the share, number of answers and traits of the very disappointed group. founder-board uses the market-first lens: is the market big enough and reachable? founder-launch and founder-plan use pull signals (repeat, referral, stock-outs) to decide whether to start spending on growth.

Example: a small coffee subscription has 60 customers. The founder surveys the 40 who received at least two boxes; 35 answer and 11 say very disappointed, 31 percent. That is below the rule of thumb, so the founder studies the 11 (all work from home and drink filter coffee) and sells to that group before adding products.

## Limits
- Rule of thumb versus evidence: the 40 percent is Ellis's own benchmark, reported second hand; no published dataset or independent validation of it was found. Andreessen's signs are an essay from experience. Use both as prompts, not guaranteed tests.
- The survey records what people say, not what they do. Pair it with repeat purchase, referral and payment data.
- The answer shifts with who is surveyed and how the offer is described; Ellis describes one company moving above 40 percent within a week by changing its messaging.
- Both sources come from fast-growth software. For a local shop the same idea shows as repeat visits and referrals.
