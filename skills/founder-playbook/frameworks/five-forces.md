# Five Forces

## The idea
Porter's framework says that how much profit a market can hold depends on its structure, not only on how good you are. Five pressures decide how the money that customers pay gets divided between the businesses in the market and everyone around them. The five are: rivalry among existing competitors, the threat of new entrants, the bargaining power of buyers, the bargaining power of suppliers, and the threat of substitutes.

Each force is a question about who can take profit away from you. Rivals compete it away through price cuts and extra spending. New entrants add capacity and push prices and costs, so low barriers to entry cap profit. Buyers take value when they are large, when your product looks like everyone else's, or when switching costs them little. Suppliers take value when few can provide a needed input or when changing is slow or costly. A substitute meets the same need in a different way, and hurts most when it is good value and easy to switch to.

The usual reading is a scorecard: rate each force weak, moderate or strong for your market and see what that says about profit potential. The second use is positional: find a spot where the forces press less on you. Structure shifts over time, so the scorecard has a date.

## When to use it
- Before committing money to an idea, to judge whether the market is likely to pay a decent margin at all.
- When choosing between two possible markets or niches for the same skills.
- When a larger customer, a single supplier or a new rival appears and you need to see what it does to your position.
- When a growth move (a new product line, a new city) puts you into a market you have not worked in.

## Questions to put to the founder
1. How many businesses sell something similar within the reach of your customers, and how many are about your size?
2. What would it cost, in money and months, for someone else to open the same business next door or online?
3. What share of your sales comes from your largest customer?
4. Can a customer switch to a competitor in one click or one visit without losing anything?
5. How many suppliers could provide your main input, and what happens to your cost if the main one raises prices by 20 percent?
6. What else could your customer spend the same money on to solve the same problem?

## Red flags
- The founder says there is no competition, which usually means substitutes or rivals have not been counted.
- One customer or one supplier accounts for most of the business.
- Anyone with a little capital and a weekend could copy the offer.
- Several rivals of equal size, slow market growth and high fixed costs: the conditions for price wars.

## How the pack uses it
founder-competitors adds a short five-forces table to founder/competitors.md, with each force rated weak, moderate or strong and one line of evidence. founder-board uses that table as input for the market-attractiveness lens in founder/board.md. founder-pricing and founder-ops read it when they set price ceilings and supplier plans.

Example: a founder plans a coffee cart near an office park. Rivalry: moderate, three cafes within 500 metres. New entrants: strong, a cart costs little to copy. Buyers: moderate, workers are individually small but switch freely. Suppliers: weak, many roasters and milk vendors. Substitutes: strong, office machines and bringing a flask. The table says margin will be squeezed, so the pack asks what the cart can offer that a rival or a flask cannot, before it signs off on the plan.

## Limits
- It describes an industry, so for a small business the answer depends on drawing the market boundary correctly: your district or niche, not the whole country.
- It rates attractiveness; it does not tell you what to do about a bad score, and a small business can still do well in a weak market by serving a narrow group well.
- It was built for established industries and large firms, and its ratings are judgement calls, so treat the output as a structured argument, not a measurement.
