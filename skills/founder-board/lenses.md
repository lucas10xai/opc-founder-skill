# The lenses

Summaries of published frameworks, in our own words. A board member applies ONE
lens and never speaks as the person the framework comes from.

Three core lenses (Offers, Monopoly, Product) sit on every board. Five guest lenses
(Lean, Moat, Customer, Operator, Calm) are added two at a time by the chair. Each
guest lens lists its "Depth": library files in the founder-playbook skill that the
member reads before writing the memo.

## The Offers lens

Source: Alex Hormozi, *$100M Offers* (2021).

**Pick the market first.** The best offer fails in a bad market. Score the market
on four things: the pain is massive, the buyers have money to spend, they are
easy to find and reach, and the market is growing. A weak market is the most
common reason this lens passes.

**The value equation.** How much a buyer values an offer goes up with the dream
outcome and the perceived likelihood of getting it, and down with the time it
takes and the effort and sacrifice it asks. Score each of the four for this
business. Most offers fail on likelihood (no proof) or effort (too much asked of
the buyer).

**Do not compete on price.** If the only difference is being cheaper, it is a
commodity. Ask what would make this so good that people feel stupid saying no:
more of the outcome, faster, with less effort, with a guarantee.

**Build the stack.** List every problem the buyer hits on the way to the outcome,
turn each into a solution, keep the ones that are cheap to deliver and valuable
to the buyer, and stack them. Then strengthen it with real scarcity or urgency
(never fake), bonuses, a guarantee and a clear name.

Questions this lens asks: Who exactly has this pain and money? Why would they
believe it works? How fast do they get a result? What is the guarantee? Could you
double the price and still be the obvious choice?

## The Monopoly lens

Source: Peter Thiel with Blake Masters, *Zero to One* (2014).

**Competition destroys profit.** A business in a crowded market fights for thin
margins. The goal is a small market you can dominate, then expand from.

**What makes a durable business:** proprietary technology or know-how roughly ten
times better than the next option on something that matters; network effects;
economies of scale; a brand. Most small businesses have none of these, so ask
which one this could build.

**The seven questions:** the engineering question (is it a breakthrough, or an
incremental improvement?), timing (why now?), monopoly (does it start with a big
share of a small market?), people (is this the right team?), distribution (is
there a way to reach buyers, not just a product?), durability (will it still hold
its position in ten years?), and the secret (what does the founder know that
others do not?).

**Distribution is half of it.** A good product with no way to reach customers is
not a business. Ask how the first 100 customers arrive and what each one costs.

Questions this lens asks: Who else sells this within reach of the same buyer?
What is the small market this can own first? What is the secret? What stops a
copycat in month six?

## The Product lens

Source: Walter Isaacson, *Steve Jobs* (2011), and the public record of how Apple
built products.

**Focus means saying no.** Most ideas do too many things. Ask what to cut until
what is left is the one thing the business must do brilliantly.

**Start from the experience and work back.** Describe the moment the customer
uses the product, then work back to what has to be true to deliver it. Not the
other way round.

**Own the whole experience.** Every touchpoint the buyer sees, from the first ad to
the receipt to the follow-up, is the product. Seams are where it breaks.

**Taste and craft, including the parts nobody sees.** Quality shows up in details;
a business that cuts corners where customers cannot look will cut them where they
can.

**Simplicity is the hard part.** If the pitch needs a paragraph, the product is not
clear yet. Ask for it in one sentence.

Questions this lens asks: What is the one thing? What does the first five minutes
with this feel like for the customer? What would you cut? Where is the experience
handed between people or systems, and what goes wrong there?

## The Lean lens (guest)

Sources: Eric Ries, *The Lean Startup* (2011); Steve Blank, *The Four Steps to the
Epiphany* (2005); Paul Graham's essays on starting a company.
Depth: `lean-startup`, `customer-development`, `graham-do-things-that-dont-scale`.

**A plan is a pile of guesses.** List the assumptions the business depends on and
find the riskiest: the one that, if wrong, makes the rest pointless. Usually it is
one of two: that this customer really has this problem and will pay to fix it, or
that the business can reach enough of them at a cost it can afford.

**Find the cheapest test that could prove it wrong.** Not a build, a test: a
conversation, a page with a price, a hand-delivered first order. Ask what the
founder can learn this week, with no more than a few days of work.

**Weigh the evidence by how costly it was to give.** An opinion costs nothing, a
promise costs little, a pre-payment costs real money, and a repeat purchase costs
money twice. Treat the ladder accordingly.

**Early on, do the work that does not scale.** The first customers should be won by
hand, with attention no scaled process would give. That is how you learn what they
actually want.

Questions this lens asks: What is the riskiest assumption? What is the cheapest
test, and what result would make you stop? Who have you actually talked to, and what
did they do rather than say? What evidence would change your mind?

## The Moat lens (guest)

Sources: Warren Buffett's shareholder letters and Charlie Munger's talks (collected
in *Poor Charlie's Almanack*); Michael Porter, *Competitive Strategy* (1980).
Depth: `moat-circle-of-competence`, `five-forces`, `inversion`.

**Who keeps the profit.** In any market, suppliers, buyers, new entrants,
substitutes and existing rivals each pull profit away. Ask which of them will win
the argument over this business's margin.

**A durable advantage, or a head start?** A moat is something that is still there
in five years and that a competitor cannot copy by spending money: a loyal base,
a location, a cost position, specialised skill, switching costs. A head start alone
is not one.

**Circle of competence.** Does the founder understand this business well enough to
know when something is wrong? Say what they know and what they would be guessing.

**Margin of safety.** Assume the plan is wrong by a third. Does the business
survive? A thin margin plus a fixed cost is how good ideas fail.

**Invert.** Ask what would make this fail, list it, and see how much of the list the
founder controls.

Questions this lens asks: What does a competitor need to beat you, and how long
would it take? What happens at one third fewer customers? What do you know about
this that a newcomer would not? What are the five most likely ways this ends, and
which can you prevent?

## The Customer lens (guest)

Sources: Colin Bryar and Bill Carr, *Working Backwards* (2021), on Amazon's method;
Clayton Christensen and colleagues on jobs to be done.
Depth: `bezos-working-backwards`, `jobs-to-be-done`.

**Work backwards from the customer.** Write the announcement a customer would read
on launch day and the questions they would ask. If it is hard to write, the idea is
not clear, or the benefit is not real.

**The job, not the person.** People buy a product to get a job done in a particular
situation. Describe the situation, what they are trying to get done, what they use
now and what makes that unsatisfying. The competitors are whatever they use now,
which may not be a business at all.

**Customer problems before features.** List the problems; check each is real by
asking who has it and how they cope today.

Questions this lens asks: What job does the customer hire this for, and in what
moment? What do they do today, and why is it not good enough? What is the headline
of the launch announcement? What are the three hardest questions in its FAQ?

## The Operator lens (guest)

Sources: Michael Gerber, *The E-Myth Revisited* (1995); Andy Grove, *High Output
Management* (1983); Sam Walton with John Huey, *Sam Walton: Made in America* (1992).
Depth: `e-myth`, `high-output-okrs`, `walton-ten-rules`.

**Can it run without a hero?** A business that works only while the founder works
is a job. Ask which routines, written down, would let someone else do each task, and
what only the founder can do today.

**Output and the number that leads it.** Name the weekly output of the business and
one early signal that predicts it. If the founder cannot name them, nobody is
steering.

**What breaks at twice the volume?** Suppliers, staff, the till, the queue, the
cash cycle. Name the first thing to snap.

**Listen to the people at the front.** Staff and customers see the problems first.
Ask how the founder will hear from them, and what they will do about it.

Questions this lens asks: What does a normal day look like, hour by hour? Which
task is done only by the founder, and what happens if they are ill for a week? What
is the first thing that breaks at double the volume? What do you measure every
week?

## The Calm lens (guest)

Sources: Paul Graham's essay on being default alive or default dead; Jason Fried and
David Heinemeier Hansson, *Rework* (2010); Mike Michalowicz, *Profit First* (2014).
Depth: `graham-default-alive`, `calm-company`, `profit-first`.

**Default alive or default dead.** If revenue and costs keep going the way they
are, does the business reach profit before the money runs out? If not, it needs
something to change, and soon.

**Small and profitable beats big and hopeful.** Charge from the first sale. Keep the
fixed costs low, so a slow month is not a crisis. Ask what the smallest version of
this business that pays the founder looks like.

**Pay profit first.** Set aside profit as money comes in, rather than hoping some is
left at the end. A business that never has a profit line has never been profitable.

**Be careful with constraints.** Fewer features, fewer hires and fewer commitments
make a business easier to run and harder to kill.

Questions this lens asks: How many months can it run on the cash, and how many to
break even? What is the smallest version that would pay you? What fixed cost could
be a variable one? When is the first real revenue, and what does it cost to get?
